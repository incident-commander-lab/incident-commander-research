Project Incident Commander
Date 9 October 2026

Incident Commander already studies what evidence to collect, and how an agent may act once policy allows it. Phase 3 asks a later question: how much authority that agent should hold, and how that authority should change when operational history proves the current boundary too wide or too narrow.

How to read this brief

This document fixes a research direction. It states the question, places it against current papers and products, and specifies the policy, the experiment, and the result that would count as success. A systematic literature review is still required before any final novelty claim. Adaptive autonomy, oversight, reliability measurement, and cost-aware evaluation are each already studied. The proposed contribution is their joint operationalization for SRE incident response.

01Research objective

Phase 1 decides which evidence to collect next during an investigation. Phase 2 extends that process into autonomous action: policy authorization, execution, verification, and audit. Both capabilities are already active subjects in research systems and commercial platforms.

Phase 3 therefore does not ask whether an AI agent can investigate an incident or remediate one. The question is how an autonomous SRE agent can safely change the amount of authority it holds over time, using demonstrated reliability, risk, cost, and verified operational outcomes.

Central idea

Autonomy is not a fixed configuration. It is a decision that can change according to evidence from previous outcomes.

02Why this problem matters now

AI agents are already deployed in production. The live question in research and industry has moved from whether agents can perform useful tasks to whether organizations can operate them reliably and economically at scale.

Production-agent evidence

The ICML 2026 paper “Measuring Agents in Production” studied 20 production-agent case studies and surveyed practitioners responsible for 86 deployed systems across 26 domains.

68%of production agents execute at most 10 steps before human intervention
70%rely primarily on prompting off-the-shelf models rather than weight tuning
74%depend primarily on human evaluation

Reliability — consistent correct behavior over time — was identified as the top development challenge. The central problem is no longer whether an agent can complete a task once. A major challenge is determining whether its behavior is reliable enough to justify continued autonomous operation.

Agent reliability evidence

The ICML 2026 paper “Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability” argues that standard benchmark accuracy is insufficient for evaluating agents. The authors introduce a 12-metric reliability profile covering consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety. Across 15 models and two benchmarks, recent capability improvements produce only small improvements in reliability.

Implication for SRE autonomy

High task accuracy does not imply readiness for autonomous production authority. An agent can show a high average success rate and still be inconsistent, fragile, unpredictable, or capable of severe failures.

03Autonomous SRE is already an active area

Autonomous incident response, by itself, is not a sufficient research gap. Diagnosis, remediation, and verification are already under study, and current benchmarks show that the tasks remain hard.

STRATUS

The NeurIPS 2025 system STRATUS — a multi-agent system for autonomous reliability engineering of modern clouds — investigates autonomous SRE on benchmarks including AIOpsLab and ITBench. The reported system achieved at least a 1.5× success-rate improvement over state-of-the-art SRE agents across multiple models. STRATUS also introduces Transactional No-Regression (TNR): system changes are evaluated so that remediation does not introduce regressions.

ITBench

IBM Research’s ITBench evaluates AI agents on real-world IT automation across SRE, cybersecurity, and FinOps. The published benchmark (ICML 2025) contains 102 scenarios. Reported state-of-the-art results remain low.

11.4%of SRE scenarios resolved by agents on state-of-the-art models (ICML 2025)
25.2%reported state of the art on CISO tasks
0%reported state of the art on FinOps tasks

Autonomous IT operations remain far from solved. The research gap should still be framed more sharply than “AI agents cannot yet solve IT incidents.”

Sharper question

Given that autonomous agents are increasingly capable, how should their operational authority be determined and adapted when their reliability is uncertain?

04Platforms already expose autonomy controls

Commercial platforms treat autonomy as an operational product concern. Microsoft’s Azure SRE Agent provides two explicit operating modes.

Review mode

Propose, then wait

The agent investigates and proposes an action, then waits for approval.

Autonomous mode

Investigate and execute

The agent investigates and executes actions without waiting for approval.

Microsoft recommends starting in review mode, observing the agent for two to four weeks, and moving specific triggers to autonomous execution once patterns are consistently approved. Autonomy can be configured per response plan or task, rather than as one global setting. Different incident types and actions need not receive the same level of autonomy.

The current platform model is largely configuration-driven: humans define when a particular trigger or response plan becomes autonomous.

Motivating question

Can the system itself learn from verified operational outcomes and adjust the autonomy boundary, rather than relying primarily on manually configured autonomy levels?

05Governance is an autonomy-level problem

Gartner’s 2026 research argues against treating AI-agent governance as binary. Gartner predicts that by 2027, 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous AI agents because of governance gaps discovered after production incidents. The research describes multiple autonomy levels, from observation to autonomous action, and argues that governance should be proportional to the agent’s autonomy and scope.

Design principle

Autonomy should be bounded by the level of trust and risk associated with the action. Proportional governance still leaves open how those boundaries should change automatically as the agent accumulates operational evidence.

06Act versus ask is already studied

Phase 3 has to acknowledge existing work on adaptive oversight. The ICML 2026 paper “The Oversight Game” models an agent that chooses to act autonomously (“PLAY”) or to defer (“ASK”). The work studies when agents should act versus request oversight, and reports reductions in safety violations in its experimental setting. A second ICML 2026 paper, “Calibrating Conservatism for Scalable Oversight,” studies how agents can learn to defer when autonomous action is less reliable.

Novelty boundary

The contribution cannot be “our agent decides whether to act or ask a human.” That problem is already under research. The Phase 3 question has to be narrower, and grounded in SRE operations.

07Autonomy is already being measured

Recent work treats autonomy itself as a measurable property. Microsoft Research’s 2026 work on agent planning for security and autonomy defines an autonomy-oriented metric from the fraction of consequential actions an agent can execute without human approval while preserving security. Autonomy can be a research objective, not only an interface setting.

The remaining opportunity for Incident Commander is to connect that idea to longitudinal operational evidence:

  • What happened when the agent acted previously?
  • Was the action correct, and was the outcome verified?
  • Did the action require rollback?
  • Did a human intervene?
  • What did the action cost, and how risky was it?

Those observations can determine whether future authority should increase, remain unchanged, or decrease.

08Cost is an independent dimension

Cost is not another word for trust. Recent evaluation research treats cost-efficiency as its own open dimension. The ACL 2026 survey “A Survey on Evaluation of LLM-based Agents” identifies gaps in cost-efficiency, safety, robustness, fine-grained evaluation, and scalable evaluation.

The ACL 2026 AgencyBench makes the scale of the cost problem concrete. It evaluates 138 tasks across 32 real-world scenarios. On average, scenarios require about 90 tool calls, about 1 million tokens, and hours of execution. The benchmark also finds substantial differences between models in resource efficiency and self-correction.

Implication for autonomous SRE

An agent should be judged not only by whether it eventually resolves an incident, but by how much investigation, computation, tool use, and operational cost it consumes. A second Phase 3 question follows: can autonomy increase while the cost of autonomous investigation and remediation stays bounded?

09The research gap

The literature already contains work on autonomous SRE, autonomous remediation, act-versus-defer decisions, safety and oversight, agent reliability, autonomy measurement, governance, and cost-efficient evaluation. The contribution should not claim that any one of these ideas is new.

The stronger gap is the combination, and its operationalization, for SRE autonomy.

Proposed gap — a hypothesis, not a novelty claim

Existing work increasingly measures agent reliability, safety, cost, and autonomy separately, while production platforms commonly configure autonomy levels by hand. A research opportunity is to test whether verified operational outcomes can dynamically adapt an SRE agent’s authority on a per-action or per-incident basis, while jointly considering reliability, risk, and cost.

A systematic literature review should still be completed before a final novelty claim.

10What adaptive autonomy means

Adaptive autonomy does not mean the language model retrains itself online. The operational policy adapts from verified outcomes. Authority becomes specific to the action and dependent on evidence. The agent is neither simply “autonomous” nor simply “not autonomous.”

Action A · evidence supports wider authority

Rollback a deployment

  • 100 rollback actions observed
  • 98 verified successful; 2 reverted
  • No harmful autonomous actions observed
  • The action is reversible and low cost

The record is strong for this action under these conditions. The policy may allow broader autonomous execution for similar incidents.

Action B · evidence keeps authority restricted

Database failover

  • 6 of 10 attempts successful
  • Several cases require intervention
  • Operational risk is high
  • Recovery is more difficult

The same autonomy level as rollback is not justified. Authority stays narrower until the record improves.

11Reliability, risk, and cost together

A broader question sits beside the main one: can reliability, risk, and cost jointly determine the appropriate autonomy level for an SRE action? The three dimensions are different properties.

Reliability

Correct under similar conditions

How often the agent makes the correct decision when the situation resembles cases already on record.

Risk

Severity if the action is wrong

How serious the consequence is, including whether the action can be reversed.

Cost

Expense of investigating and acting

Tool use, computation, time, and operational disruption.

Action Reliability Risk Cost Potential autonomy
Roll back a recent deployment High Low / medium Low High
Restart an unhealthy service High Medium Low High / medium
Database failover Medium / high High Medium Restricted
Irreversible infrastructure change High Very high Variable Very low / denied
Investigate an unknown failure Uncertain Low at first High Limited investigation
The decision to study

Given demonstrated reliability, the consequences of failure, and the cost of acting or investigating, what level of autonomy is justified? Confidence alone is not the decision.

12Proposed research question

On the current literature and industry evidence, the strongest candidate question is:

Phase 3 research question

How can an autonomous SRE agent dynamically adapt its operational authority based on demonstrated reliability, action risk, cost, and verified outcomes?

A shorter line, suitable for a talk:

Presentation form

Can SRE autonomy adapt from verified operational outcomes?

The working hypothesis:

Hypothesis

An SRE agent can safely expand or reduce its autonomy when autonomy decisions are based on demonstrated reliability, risk, cost, and verified operational outcomes, rather than on a fixed autonomy setting.

13How this extends Incident Commander

The three phases form one research progression. Phase 3 is the authority decision that follows evidence selection and authorized action. It uses historical verified outcomes to adapt operational autonomy.

Phase 1 · Evidence decision

What next?

What should I investigate next?

The system optimizes evidence collection.

Phase 2 · Action decision

May I act?

Do I have enough evidence to act, and is the action allowed?

Investigate, decide, authorize, act, verify, and audit.

Phase 3 · Authority decision

How much authority?

Given what has happened before, how much authority should I have for this type of action now?

Adapt autonomy from verified operational history.

14Proposed architecture

The existing Incident Commander loop extends through outcome measurement into an explicit update of the autonomy boundary. The final step updates an autonomy policy. It does not modify the underlying model weights.

Detect Investigate Decide Authorize Act Verify Measure outcome Update history Adjust boundary

Teal marks the Phase 3 extension. Detect through verify is the existing loop.

Possible policy outputs for a given action and context:

Increase autonomy Maintain current autonomy Restrict autonomy Require additional evidence Escalate Deny

15Experimental design

The strongest experiment compares two systems on the same incident scenarios.

Baseline

Fixed autonomy

The same autonomy policy is used throughout the experiment.

Proposed

Adaptive autonomy

The system adjusts autonomy from verified historical outcomes.

Measure both systems on the same scenarios:

  1. 01Incident resolution rate
  2. 02Root-cause accuracy
  3. 03Harmful-action rate
  4. 04Rollback / reversion rate
  5. 05Human intervention rate
  6. 06Number of autonomous actions
  7. 07Investigation cost
  8. 08Tool calls
  9. 09LLM tokens
  10. 10Time to resolution
  11. 11Calibration / reliability
  12. 12Autonomy achieved over time
Trade-off under test

Can adaptive autonomy increase autonomous coverage without increasing harmful actions, while keeping operational cost bounded?

16What counts as success

A successful Phase 3 result is not “the agent became more autonomous.” That outcome can be dangerous. The stronger result is that the adaptive system increased autonomous coverage while holding or improving safety and reliability, without a disproportionate rise in investigation or execution cost.

Signal Illustrative movement
Autonomous coverageIncrease
Human interventionsDecrease
Resolution rateIncrease, or hold
Harmful actionsNear zero
Unreverted failuresNear zero
Cost per incidentDecrease, or stay bounded
Reliability and calibrationHold, or improve

The pattern above is a signature of success, not a set of thresholds. Exact thresholds should be set from the experiment.

17Novelty constraint

The project should not claim that adaptive autonomy is unresearched. That claim is too broad. Existing research already covers adaptive oversight, act-versus-defer decisions, autonomy metrics, reliability evaluation, safety-aware autonomy, cost-aware agent evaluation, and governance by autonomy level.

Outside the claim

Too broad to defend

That no prior work studies adaptive autonomy, oversight, reliability, autonomy metrics, or cost-aware evaluation.

Defensible contribution

Specific setting and combination

SRE incident-response autonomy that adapts authority from verified operational outcomes, while explicitly using reliability, risk, reversibility, and cost.

Novelty should be established by a systematic literature review and by comparison with the closest papers.

19Research position

Autonomous SRE is moving from experimentation toward production deployment. Building an autonomous SRE agent is already being addressed by research and by commercial platforms. The more interesting next question is how such an agent should earn, maintain, or lose operational authority.

The proposed system remains autonomous by default. Its authority stays bounded by policy, and is informed over time by demonstrated reliability, action risk, reversibility, operational cost, verified outcomes, and the history of intervention and rollback.

Hypothesis

Autonomy should be adaptive rather than fixed. An SRE agent should gain broader authority when verified operational history shows that the additional autonomy is justified, and lose authority when the evidence shows that it is not.

That extends Incident Commander from evidence selection, to autonomous remediation, to adaptive autonomy. The project’s core question moves from whether an agent can act autonomously to whether an agent can safely determine and adapt how much autonomy it should have.
